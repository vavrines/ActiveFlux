\begin{abstract}
It is challenging to construct high-order Boltzmann solvers that remain compact in physical space and preserve the conservative structure of particle transport and collision. Taking the full Boltzmann equation as the governing model, this paper develops an active flux kinetic formulation on one- and two-dimensional Cartesian domains. Velocity discretization produces a coupled system in which every molecular-velocity component undergoes linear physical transport while all components remain coupled through the nonlinear collision integral. Active flux is applied to this physical-space system: cell averages and shared boundary point values define a continuous reconstruction, evolved boundary data provide space--time kinetic fluxes without a Riemann solver, and the collision operator is evaluated locally in physical space with a conservative velocity-space discretization. A quadratic reconstruction is used in one dimension and a continuous biparabolic reconstruction in two dimensions. A complementary method-of-lines formulation advances the same degrees of freedom with explicit Runge--Kutta or implicit--explicit Runge--Kutta methods. Fourier analysis of the transport operator identifies a third-order physical branch and a strongly damped internal branch associated with the additional interface degree of freedom; the fully discrete spectrum also quantifies the molecular-velocity-dependent phase and amplitude errors. The collision interface is formulated first for the Boltzmann integral, with conservative fast spectral evaluation and moment projection. The Bhatnagar--Gross--Krook model is introduced as a simplified collision specialization for verification, asymptotic analysis, and penalization. A formal hydrodynamic-limit argument identifies the requirements for an Euler-limit asymptotic-preserving realization and motivates a penalized treatment of the full Boltzmann operator. The numerical study is organized around four cases: accuracy tests, the Sod problem, normal shock structure, and a two-dimensional lid-driven cavity. One-dimensional BGK tests identify third-order transport and density convergence. The Sod problem is then solved with a three-dimensional molecular-velocity grid and the full fast-spectral Boltzmann collision integral at reference Knudsen numbers $10^{-4}$, $10^{-2}$, and $1$. A preliminary normal-shock calculation compares full Boltzmann and BGK collisions and shows that heat flux distinguishes them earlier than the conserved macroscopic profiles. Steady shock refinement, the cavity, and uniform-in-Knudsen asymptotic validation remain to be completed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Gaseous flows possess a multi-scale structure. At the molecular mean-free-path scale, the Boltzmann equation describes the transport and collision of particles through the evolution of a probability density function. At hydrodynamic scales, its asymptotic limits recover the familiar equations of continuum fluid mechanics. This connection provides a unified physical description, but it also introduces high dimensionality, nonlinearity, and stiffness into numerical simulation. Relaxation equations, among which the Bhatnagar--Gross--Krook (BGK) model is the most established example, reduce the collision integral while retaining conservation and the approach to a Maxwellian equilibrium \cite{bhatnagar1954model,cercignani1988boltzmann}.

High-order kinetic methods are attractive because phase-space discretization produces many degrees of freedom even on a modest mesh. Improving the accuracy achieved per physical cell can therefore reduce both memory consumption and collision cost. Existing constructions include discontinuous Galerkin, flux reconstruction, semi-Lagrangian, and high-order finite-volume formulations. A flux reconstruction kinetic scheme has demonstrated that compact polynomial representations and kinetic upwinding can be combined with the full Boltzmann collision integral \cite{xiao2021flux}. Scale-dependent kinetic fluxes have also been incorporated into stochastic Galerkin and collocation settings for uncertain multi-scale flow transport \cite{xiao2021stochastic}. More recently, differentiable programming has coupled kinetic solvers with gradient-based model discovery and numerical optimization \cite{xiao2025differentiable}. These developments share the objective of embedding physical structure into algorithms that remain flexible for scientific computing.

The active flux method offers a different route to compact high-order discretization. It evolves cell averages together with point values located at cell interfaces. The additional values define a globally continuous reconstruction and provide the interface flux directly after an evolution step. Eymann and Roe introduced the formulation for hyperbolic systems and explored schemes up to third order without enlarging the stencil \cite{eymann2011active}. Subsequent studies clarified the construction for two-dimensional Cartesian grids, nonlinear problems, balance laws, and arbitrary order extensions \cite{barsukow2019cartesian,barsukow2021nonlinear,barsukow2021source,abgrall2023extensions}. The combination of conservative averages and independently evolved boundary values is particularly appealing for kinetic equations: at every molecular velocity, particle transport is a scalar advection problem with a known characteristic direction.

Active flux methods have recently been used for kinetic transport in the Vlasov--Poisson system, where a compact third-order discretization and a positivity-preserving limiter were developed \cite{kiechle2025positivity}. Collisional gas dynamics introduces a different numerical structure. The Boltzmann collision operator is a nonlinear, velocity-space integral that is local in physical space, whereas its BGK approximation is a nonlinear relaxation toward an equilibrium determined by velocity moments. A truncated velocity discretization can also violate the collision invariants unless its discrete moments are corrected. These issues must be handled without destroying the compact physical-space reconstruction.

In this paper, an active flux kinetic framework is constructed for the Boltzmann equation in one and two physical dimensions. The derivation starts from the discrete-velocity Boltzmann system, not from a relaxation equation: active flux discretizes physical transport, while the local velocity-space collision routine is a replaceable module. BGK is invoked only when an inexpensive collision model, an exact relaxation map, or a controlled verification problem is useful. The main elements are summarized as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The active flux reconstruction and characteristic evolution are applied independently at every discrete molecular velocity. The one-dimensional quadratic reconstruction is extended to two-dimensional Cartesian cells through a continuous biparabolic polynomial determined by one cell average and eight shared boundary values.
  \item Evolved corner and edge-midpoint values are integrated with a space--time Simpson rule to provide a conservative third-order transport update in two dimensions.
  \item The collision interface is designed to accept either the full Boltzmann operator, evaluated by a fast spectral method, or the BGK relaxation model. Tensor-product physical-space quadrature retains the spatial order of the nonlinear collision update.
  \item A discrete moment projection enforces the collision invariants of the full operator. For BGK relaxation, a corrected discrete Maxwellian reproduces the same quadrature moments.
  \item The active flux evolution is also expressed as a semi-discrete right-hand side for the combined cell-average and boundary-point state. The same operator can therefore be advanced by explicit Runge--Kutta methods or partitioned into transport and collision for implicit--explicit integration.
  \item A two-branch Fourier analysis separates the propagating physical mode from the damped internal active flux mode. Modified-wavenumber expansions and fully discrete amplification factors quantify transport dissipation, dispersion, and the Runge--Kutta CFL restriction at every molecular velocity.
  \item The hydrodynamic limit is analyzed at fixed mesh spacing and time step. A degree-of-freedom equilibrium projection gives the BGK collision operator an exact discrete null space, while a stiffly accurate implicit--explicit scheme removes the collision-time stability restriction. A penalized construction supplies the corresponding target for the full Boltzmann operator.
  \item The numerical programme contains four cases of increasing complexity: accuracy tests, the Sod problem, normal shock structure, and a two-dimensional lid-driven cavity. The current Julia results cover one-dimensional BGK accuracy tests, a full-Boltzmann Sod calculation at three reference Knudsen numbers, and a preliminary full-Boltzmann/BGK normal-shock comparison; the two-dimensional cavity remains the principal multidimensional validation target.
\end{itemize}

The implementation uses the data structures and kinetic-theory functions of \texttt{KitBase.jl}, which forms part of the open-source \texttt{Kinetic.jl} ecosystem \cite{xiao2021kinetic}. The semi-discrete operators follow the in-place problem interface of \texttt{OrdinaryDiffEq.jl}, so the spatial discretization is not tied to one time integrator \cite{rackauckas2017differentialequations}. Julia provides the multiple-dispatch and generic-programming facilities used to keep the numerical formulation close to its mathematical expression \cite{bezanson2017julia}.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:kinetic} presents the Boltzmann equation, its BGK specialization, and the velocity moments. Section~\ref{sec:algorithm} describes the one- and two-dimensional active flux reconstructions, semi-discrete and characteristic transport forms, transport-spectrum analysis, collision update, asymptotic analysis, and discontinuity treatment. Section~\ref{sec:numerical} presents the four numerical cases, with completed results and planned validation clearly distinguished. The last section is the conclusion.

\section{Kinetic theory}
\label{sec:kinetic}

The Boltzmann equation tracks the temporal-spatial evolution of the particle distribution function $f(t,\vct{x},\vct{v})$, where $\vct{x}\in\Omega_x\subset\R^{d_x}$, $\vct{v}\in\R^{d_v}$, and $d_x\in\{1,2\}$. Denote by $\vct{v}_x=(v_1,\ldots,v_{d_x})^T$ the molecular-velocity components associated with physical transport. In the absence of an external force, the equation reads
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}
  + \vct{v}_x\cdot\nabla_{\vct{x}} f
  = \mathcal{Q}(f,f),
  \label{eq:boltzmann}
\end{equation}
where the full collision operator is
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{Q}(f,f)(\vct{v})
  ={}& \int_{\R^{d_v}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d_v-1}}
  B\bigl(|\vct{g}|,\vct{\sigma}\cdot\widehat{\vct{g}}\bigr)
  \left(f'f_*'-ff_*\right)\dd\vct{\sigma}\dd\vct{v}_*,
  \label{eq:collision-operator}\\
  \vct{v}'={}&\frac{\vct{v}+\vct{v}_*}{2}
  +\frac{|\vct{g}|}{2}\vct{\sigma},
  \qquad
  \vct{v}_*'=\frac{\vct{v}+\vct{v}_*}{2}
  -\frac{|\vct{g}|}{2}\vct{\sigma},
  \qquad
  \vct{g}=\vct{v}-\vct{v}_*.
  \label{eq:post-collision}
\end{align}
Here, $B$ is the collision kernel and primes denote post-collision distributions. The operator is local in $(t,\vct{x})$ but nonlocal and quadratic in molecular velocity. The macroscopic conservative variables are velocity moments of $f$,
\begin{equation}
  \vct{W}
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \rho \\
    \rho\vct{U} \\
    \rho E
  \end{pmatrix}
  = \int_{\R^{d_v}} f\vct{\psi}\dd\vct{v},
  \qquad
  \vct{\psi}(\vct{v})
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 \\
    \vct{v} \\
    |\vct{v}|^2/2
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \label{eq:moments}
\end{equation}
The vector $\vct{\psi}$ contains the collision invariants. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
  \int_{\R^{d_v}} \mathcal{Q}(f,f)\vct{\psi}\dd\vct{v} = \vct{0}.
  \label{eq:compatibility}
\end{equation}

The velocity domain is discretized by points $\vct{v}_{\vct{j}}$ and quadrature weights $\omega_{\vct{j}}$, where $\vct{j}$ is a velocity-space multi-index. Writing $\vct{f}=(f_{\vct{j}})_{\vct{j}}$, the primary kinetic system considered by the numerical method is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial f_{\vct{j}}}{\partial t}
  +\vct{v}_{x,\vct{j}}\cdot\nabla_{\vct{x}}f_{\vct{j}}
  =\mathcal{Q}_{h,\vct{j}}(\vct{f},\vct{f}),
  \qquad \text{for every }\vct{j},
  \label{eq:discrete-velocity-boltzmann}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{Q}_{h,\vct{j}}$ denotes a consistent discrete Boltzmann collision operator. Although the transport equation is scalar at a fixed $\vct{j}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete-velocity-boltzmann} remains a nonlinear coupled system because each collision value depends on the complete velocity vector $\vct{f}$. The semi-discrete conservative variables are
\begin{equation}
  \vct{W}_h = \sum_{\vct{j}} \omega_{\vct{j}}
  f_{\vct{j}}\vct{\psi}_{\vct{j}}.
  \label{eq:discrete-moments}
\end{equation}
All macroscopic diagnostics and collision-compatibility corrections are evaluated with the same quadrature. The desired discrete analogue of Eq.~\eqref{eq:compatibility} is
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\vct{j}}\omega_{\vct{j}}
  \mathcal{Q}_{h,\vct{j}}\vct{\psi}_{\vct{j}}=\vct{0},
  \label{eq:discrete-collision-compatibility}
\end{equation}
which will be enforced by the projection in Section~\ref{sec:collision-conservation}. The active flux construction in Section~\ref{sec:algorithm} is applied directly to Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete-velocity-boltzmann}; consequently it does not rely on a Maxwellian form of the collision operator.

The BGK model is introduced only as a computationally inexpensive specialization. It replaces the Boltzmann collision integral with relaxation toward the Maxwellian distribution \cite{bhatnagar1954model},
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}
  + \vct{v}_x\cdot\nabla_{\vct{x}}f
  = \frac{\mathcal{M}[f]-f}{\tau},
  \label{eq:bgk}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is the collision time. In $d_v$ molecular-velocity dimensions,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{M}(\vct{v};\rho,\vct{U},\lambda)
  = \rho\left(\frac{\lambda}{\pi}\right)^{d_v/2}
    \exp\left[-\lambda|\vct{v}-\vct{U}|^2\right],
  \label{eq:maxwellian}
\end{equation}
with $p=\rho/(2\lambda)$. In the notation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete-velocity-boltzmann}, this specialization amounts to replacing $\mathcal{Q}_{h,\vct{j}}$ by a conservative discrete approximation of $(\mathcal{M}[\vct{f}]-f_{\vct{j}})/\tau$; none of the active flux transport degrees of freedom or reconstructions is changed. The physical dimension $d_x$ and velocity dimension $d_v$ are conceptually independent, and the full Boltzmann formulation normally retains a multidimensional velocity space even for a one-dimensional physical problem. The accuracy programs use a single distribution in one physical and one molecular-velocity dimension, denoted \texttt{1d1f1v}; with no reduced internal-energy distribution, $K=0$ and $\gamma=3$. The Sod and normal-shock calculations instead retain all three molecular-velocity components, denoted \texttt{1d1f3v}, and represent a monatomic gas with $K=0$ and $\gamma=5/3$.

\section{Solution algorithm}
\label{sec:algorithm}

\subsection{Active flux discretization of the discrete-velocity Boltzmann equation}
\label{sec:active-flux-construction}

The starting point is the discrete-velocity Boltzmann system in Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete-velocity-boltzmann}. Its two numerical operations have different structures. Physical transport is diagonal in molecular velocity: for a fixed $\vct{v}_{\vct{j}}$, it is a scalar advection operator with known propagation direction. Collision is local in physical space but couples the complete vector $\vct{f}$. The active flux discretization is therefore constructed in physical space independently for every $\vct{j}$, while every collision call receives all velocity components at the same physical location. This separation is the reason the same active flux representation can be combined with a fast spectral Boltzmann operator, a direct collision discretization, or the BGK specialization without changing its transport stencil.

\subsubsection{Degrees of freedom and continuous reconstruction}

Let $K$ be a physical cell with measure $|K|$, boundary $\partial K$, and a prescribed set of boundary nodes $\mathcal{S}(K)$. For every discrete molecular velocity, active flux retains two types of unknown,
\begin{equation}
  \avg{f}_{K,\vct{j}}(t)
  =\frac{1}{|K|}\int_K f(t,\vct{x},\vct{v}_{\vct{j}})\dd\vct{x},
  \qquad
  f_{\sigma,\vct{j}}(t)
  \approx f(t,\vct{x}_{\sigma},\vct{v}_{\vct{j}}),
  \quad \sigma\in\mathcal{S}(K).
  \label{eq:active-flux-dofs}
\end{equation}
The first quantity is conservative and belongs to one cell. A boundary value is stored only once and is shared by all cells meeting at $\vct{x}_{\sigma}$. For cell $K$, these data define a polynomial $p_{K,\vct{j}}=\mathcal{R}_K(\avg{f}_{K,\vct{j}},\{f_{\sigma,\vct{j}}\})$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{|K|}\int_K p_{K,\vct{j}}(\vct{x})\dd\vct{x}
  =\avg{f}_{K,\vct{j}},
  \qquad
  p_{K,\vct{j}}(\vct{x}_{\sigma})=f_{\sigma,\vct{j}}
  \quad\text{for }\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(K).
  \label{eq:active-flux-reconstruction-conditions}
\end{equation}
The boundary nodes are chosen so that neighboring reconstructions have the same trace on their common face. Hence $p_{h,\vct{j}}$ is continuous across the physical mesh, although its normal derivative can be discontinuous. This differs from a standard finite-volume reconstruction: the interface trace is an independently evolved degree of freedom rather than two extrapolated states. Consequently the kinetic flux is obtained directly from one shared distribution value; a Riemann solver is neither required nor introduced.

At $t=0$, cell averages are initialized by physical-space quadrature of $f_0(\vct{x},\vct{v}_{\vct{j}})$, whereas boundary values are sampled directly at $\vct{x}_{\sigma}$. All molecular velocities use the same physical nodes. It is therefore useful to regard the reconstructed state at one physical point as the vector
\begin{equation}
  \vct{p}_K(\vct{x})
  =\left(p_{K,\vct{j}}(\vct{x})\right)_{\vct{j}}.
  \label{eq:reconstructed-velocity-vector}
\end{equation}
Transport accesses its components separately, while the Boltzmann collision routine evaluates $\mathcal{Q}_h(\vct{p}_K,\vct{p}_K)$ as a coupled velocity-space operation.

\subsubsection{Boundary evolution, kinetic flux, and cell balance}

The boundary values must be evolved in addition to the cell averages. At a shared node $\sigma$, the transport derivative is taken from the cell lying upstream with respect to $\vct{v}_{x,\vct{j}}$. Denoting this one-sided derivative by $\vct{D}^{\mathrm{up}}_{\sigma,\vct{j}}$, the unsplit point-value equation is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd f_{\sigma,\vct{j}}}{\dd t}
  =-\vct{v}_{x,\vct{j}}\cdot
    \vct{D}^{\mathrm{up}}_{\sigma,\vct{j}}
  +\mathcal{Q}_{h,\vct{j}}
   (\vct{f}_{\sigma},\vct{f}_{\sigma}),
  \qquad
  \vct{f}_{\sigma}=(f_{\sigma,\vct{j}})_{\vct{j}}.
  \label{eq:general-point-evolution}
\end{equation}
The first term is scalar and upwind at each velocity; the second term couples the velocity vector but remains local to the node. In a characteristic split formulation, the transport part is evaluated by tracing $\vct{x}_{\sigma}-s\vct{v}_{x,\vct{j}}$ into the upwind reconstruction, and collision is advanced by a separate local map. In the method-of-lines formulation, both terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-point-evolution} enter the same additive right-hand side or the two components of an implicit--explicit problem. These are temporal choices applied to the same active flux spatial data.

For an oriented face $e\subset\partial K$ with outward normal $\vct{n}_{K,e}$, evolved boundary values determine the time- and face-averaged kinetic flux
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{K,e,\vct{j}}
  =\frac{1}{\Delta t\,|e|}
   \int_0^{\Delta t}\int_e
   \left(\vct{v}_{x,\vct{j}}\cdot\vct{n}_{K,e}\right)
   f_{\partial,\vct{j}}(t^n+s,\vct{x})
   \dd s_e\dd s.
  \label{eq:general-space-time-flux}
\end{equation}
Here $f_{\partial,\vct{j}}$ is the continuous boundary trace assembled from the evolved active flux nodes. The same value is used with opposite normals by the two neighboring cells, so cancellation of interior face contributions is exact.

The nonlinear collision source of a cell average must be averaged after reconstructing the distribution, not formed by applying $\mathcal{Q}_h$ directly to $\avg{\vct{f}}_K$. Formally, its space--time average is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{K,\vct{j}}
  =\frac{1}{\Delta t\,|K|}
   \int_0^{\Delta t}\int_K
   \mathcal{Q}_{h,\vct{j}}
   (\vct{p}_K(t^n+s,\vct{x}),
    \vct{p}_K(t^n+s,\vct{x}))
   \dd\vct{x}\dd s.
  \label{eq:cell-averaged-collision}
\end{equation}
Physical-space Gaussian quadrature and the selected temporal integrator approximate this expression. Combining transport and collision gives the conservative cell update
\begin{equation}
  \avg{f}_{K,\vct{j}}^{,n+1}
  =\avg{f}_{K,\vct{j}}^{,n}
  -\frac{\Delta t}{|K|}
   \sum_{e\subset\partial K}|e|
   \widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{K,e,\vct{j}}
  +\Delta t\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{K,\vct{j}}.
  \label{eq:general-active-flux-update}
\end{equation}
Global conservation follows by summing Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-active-flux-update} over physical cells and then taking the discrete velocity moments: internal transport fluxes cancel pairwise, and the projected Boltzmann collision term has zero invariant moments by Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete-collision-compatibility}. The BGK model changes only the evaluation of $\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{K,\vct{j}}$.

\subsubsection{Complete solution procedure}

One time step of the general method consists of the following operations.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Discretize molecular velocity and prepare the collision backend. For the target Boltzmann solver this includes the fast spectral convolution data and invariant projection; selecting BGK replaces only this backend.
  \item Initialize or retrieve the cell-average and shared-boundary vectors for all $\vct{j}$, and impose the incoming part of each physical boundary condition according to the sign of $\vct{v}_{x,\vct{j}}\cdot\vct{n}$.
  \item Construct $p_{K,\vct{j}}$ in every physical cell and, when collision is evaluated, assemble the full vector $\vct{p}_K$ at every physical-space quadrature point.
  \item Evolve every shared boundary value from the upwind reconstruction. The characteristic path evaluates the foot of the molecular trajectory; the method-of-lines path evaluates the one-sided derivative in Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-point-evolution} at every Runge--Kutta stage.
  \item Integrate the evolved boundary trace in time and along each face to obtain Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-space-time-flux}. In one dimension this time integral is analytic for the quadratic reconstruction; in two dimensions the face and time integrals use tensor Simpson quadrature.
  \item Evaluate the collision operator on each reconstructed velocity vector, enforce its discrete invariant moments, and integrate the result in physical space. A split method advances these local collision problems before and after transport; an unsplit method adds their stage derivatives to the transport right-hand side.
  \item Update all cell averages by the conservative balance in Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-active-flux-update} and retain the independently evolved boundary values as the second set of solution unknowns.
  \item Apply the positivity or shock limiter when required, refresh boundary ghosts, and compute macroscopic variables with the same velocity quadrature as Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete-moments}.
\end{enumerate}
This sequence exposes precisely which components are universal and which are model dependent. The active flux reconstruction, point transport, and conservative face balance depend only on $\vct{v}_{x,\vct{j}}$. The collision backend, its conservation correction, and its stiff temporal treatment contain the distinction between the Boltzmann equation and BGK.

\subsubsection{One-dimensional quadratic realization}

Consider now a uniform one-dimensional mesh with cells $I_i=[x_{i-1/2},x_{i+1/2}]$ and spacing $\Delta x$. For every molecular velocity $v_j$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:active-flux-dofs} reduces to the cell average
\begin{equation}
  \avg{f}_{i,j}(t)
  = \frac{1}{\Delta x}\int_{I_i} f(t,x,v_j)\dd x
  \label{eq:cell-average}
\end{equation}
and the shared interface values $f_{i-1/2,j}$ and $f_{i+1/2,j}$. On the local coordinate $\xi=(x-x_{i-1/2})/\Delta x\in[0,1]$, these three quantities determine the quadratic reconstruction
\begin{align}
  p_{i,j}(\xi)
  ={}& (3\xi^2-4\xi+1)f_{i-1/2,j}
  +(6\xi-6\xi^2)\avg{f}_{i,j} \nonumber\\
  &+(3\xi^2-2\xi)f_{i+1/2,j}.
  \label{eq:quadratic-reconstruction}
\end{align}
Direct substitution gives $p_{i,j}(0)=f_{i-1/2,j}$, $p_{i,j}(1)=f_{i+1/2,j}$, and $\int_0^1p_{i,j}(\xi)\dd\xi=\avg{f}_{i,j}$. Thus it satisfies both conditions in Eq.~\eqref{eq:active-flux-reconstruction-conditions}. Since each interface value is shared by adjacent cells, the global reconstruction is continuous even though its derivative is generally discontinuous. The two one-sided derivatives of this polynomial supply the kinetic upwind derivative in the semi-discrete point equation, while its values along molecular characteristics provide the fully discrete update derived in Section~\ref{sec:characteristic-transport}.

\subsection{Two-dimensional Cartesian representation}

On a rectangular cell $C_{i,k}$, the active flux degrees of freedom at a fixed molecular velocity consist of the cell average and eight boundary point values: four values at the vertices and four at the edge midpoints. These point values are shared by every adjacent cell that contains the corresponding boundary location. This is the Cartesian active flux arrangement introduced for multidimensional systems in Ref.~\cite{barsukow2019cartesian}.

Let $(\xi,\eta)\in[0,1]^2$ be the reference coordinates and introduce the quadratic nodal functions
\begin{equation}
  \ell_0(s)=2s^2-3s+1,
  \qquad
  \ell_1(s)=4s(1-s),
  \qquad
  \ell_2(s)=2s^2-s.
  \label{eq:quadratic-nodal-basis}
\end{equation}
The boundary values are denoted by $f_{ab,\vct{j}}$ at the tensor nodes $(a/2,b/2)$, where $a,b\in\{0,1,2\}$ and $(a,b)\ne(1,1)$. The biparabolic reconstruction is
\begin{equation}
  p_{i,k,\vct{j}}(\xi,\eta)
  =\sum_{a=0}^{2}\sum_{b=0}^{2}
  f_{ab,\vct{j}}\ell_a(\xi)\ell_b(\eta),
  \label{eq:biparabolic-reconstruction}
\end{equation}
where the auxiliary center coefficient is determined by the cell average,
\begin{equation}
  f_{11,\vct{j}}
  =\frac{1}{m_1^2}
  \left[
  \avg{f}_{i,k,\vct{j}}
  -\mathop{\sum_{a=0}^{2}\sum_{b=0}^{2}}_{(a,b)\ne(1,1)}
  m_am_bf_{ab,\vct{j}}
  \right],
  \qquad
  (m_0,m_1,m_2)=\left(\frac16,\frac23,\frac16\right).
  \label{eq:biparabolic-center}
\end{equation}
The weights $m_a=\int_0^1\ell_a(s)\dd s$ make the cell average of Eq.~\eqref{eq:biparabolic-reconstruction} equal to $\avg{f}_{i,k,\vct{j}}$. On a common edge, the reconstructions from the two neighboring cells reduce to quadratic polynomials that interpolate the same two vertices and midpoint. They therefore coincide along the complete edge, yielding a globally continuous reconstruction.

\subsection{Characteristic transport}
\label{sec:characteristic-transport}

During a collisionless substep, every velocity component satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t f_{\vct{j}}
  +\vct{v}_{x,\vct{j}}\cdot\nabla_{\vct{x}}f_{\vct{j}}=0.
  \label{eq:transport}
\end{equation}
The exact characteristic solution is $f_{\vct{j}}(t+\Delta t,\vct{x})=f_{\vct{j}}(t,\vct{x}-\vct{v}_{x,\vct{j}}\Delta t)$. In one dimension, for $v_j>0$, the characteristic arriving at $x_{i+1/2}$ originates from cell $i$. Let
\begin{equation}
  c_j = \frac{|v_j|\Delta t}{\Delta x},
  \qquad 0\le c_j\le 1.
  \label{eq:cfl}
\end{equation}
Evaluating Eq.~\eqref{eq:quadratic-reconstruction} at the characteristic foot gives
\begin{align}
  f_{i+1/2,j}^{n+1}
  ={}& f_{i+1/2,j}^{n}
  -c_j(3c_j-2)\left(\avg{f}_{i,j}^{n}-f_{i-1/2,j}^{n}\right) \nonumber\\
  &-c_j(4-3c_j)\left(f_{i+1/2,j}^{n}-\avg{f}_{i,j}^{n}\right).
  \label{eq:point-update-positive}
\end{align}
The exact time average of the interface distribution over the step is
\begin{align}
  \avg{f}_{i+1/2,j}^{\,t}
  ={}& f_{i+1/2,j}^{n}
  -c_j(c_j-1)\left(\avg{f}_{i,j}^{n}-f_{i-1/2,j}^{n}\right) \nonumber\\
  &-c_j(2-c_j)\left(f_{i+1/2,j}^{n}-\avg{f}_{i,j}^{n}\right).
  \label{eq:time-average-positive}
\end{align}
For $v_j<0$, the same construction is reflected and uses cell $i+1$. The cell average is advanced conservatively,
\begin{equation}
  \avg{f}_{i,j}^{n+1}
  = \avg{f}_{i,j}^{n}
  -\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}v_j
  \left(\avg{f}_{i+1/2,j}^{\,t}
       -\avg{f}_{i-1/2,j}^{\,t}\right).
  \label{eq:conservative-update}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:point-update-positive}--\eqref{eq:conservative-update} constitute a one-step, third-order active flux transport operator. The molecular velocity supplies the upwind direction directly, so no approximate Riemann solver is needed.

In two dimensions, the same characteristic construction is applied to every vertex and edge midpoint. For a boundary point $\vct{x}_{e,r}$, temporal nodes $\vartheta_q\in\{0,1/2,1\}$, and the cell $C(e,r,\vct{j},q)$ containing the characteristic foot, the evolved point value is
\begin{equation}
  f_{e,r,\vct{j}}^{n+\vartheta_q}
  =p_{C(e,r,\vct{j},q),\vct{j}}^n
  \left(\vct{x}_{e,r}-\vartheta_q\Delta t\vct{v}_{x,\vct{j}}\right).
  \label{eq:two-dimensional-point-update}
\end{equation}
The edge nodes $r=0,1,2$ are the two vertices and midpoint. With Simpson weights $\sigma=(1,4,1)/6$, the space--time average of the interface distribution is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{f}_{e,\vct{j}}
  =\sum_{q=0}^{2}\sum_{r=0}^{2}
  \sigma_q\sigma_r f_{e,r,\vct{j}}^{n+\vartheta_q}.
  \label{eq:space-time-simpson}
\end{equation}
The conservative update of the cell average becomes
\begin{equation}
  \avg{f}_{C,\vct{j}}^{n+1}
  =\avg{f}_{C,\vct{j}}^{n}
  -\frac{\Delta t}{|C|}
  \sum_{e\subset\partial C}|e|
  \left(\vct{v}_{x,\vct{j}}\cdot\vct{n}_e\right)
  \widehat{f}_{e,\vct{j}}.
  \label{eq:two-dimensional-conservative-update}
\end{equation}
The tensor Simpson rule is third-order accurate in space and time for the evolved boundary data \cite{barsukow2019cartesian}. A compact nearest-neighbor evolution is obtained under the sufficient restriction $|v_{1,\vct{j}}|\Delta t\le\Delta x/2$ and $|v_{2,\vct{j}}|\Delta t\le\Delta y/2$; a larger displacement can instead be handled by locating the characteristic foot in a wider stencil.

\subsection{Semi-discrete formulation and Runge--Kutta integration}
\label{sec:semidiscrete}

The characteristic formulas above define a compact fully discrete transport operator. A second formulation is useful when the temporal method should be selected independently of the physical-space discretization. Collect the cell averages and shared boundary point values at every molecular velocity into one vector $\vct{F}_h$. After velocity and physical-space discretization, the kinetic equation is written in method-of-lines form,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd\vct{F}_h}{\dd t}
  =\mathcal{R}_h(\vct{F}_h)
  =\mathcal{T}_h(\vct{F}_h)
  +\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{C}_h(\vct{F}_h),
  \label{eq:semidiscrete-system}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon$ is proportional to the Knudsen number. The transport operator is independent of $\varepsilon$. In the general formulation, $\mathcal{C}_h$ is the conservative normalized Boltzmann collision operator; replacing it by BGK relaxation gives the simplified model without modifying $\mathcal{T}_h$ or the active flux state.

In one dimension, the cell-average component remains a conservative flux difference,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd\avg{f}_{i,j}}{\dd t}
  =-\frac{v_j}{\Delta x}
  \left(f_{i+1/2,j}-f_{i-1/2,j}\right)
  +\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\avg{\mathcal{C}}_{i,j}.
  \label{eq:semidiscrete-cell-average}
\end{equation}
The point-value transport derivative follows directly from the one-sided derivative of the continuous quadratic reconstruction. At $x_{i+1/2}$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd f_{i+1/2,j}}{\dd t}
  =-v_jD_{i+1/2,j}^{\mathrm{up}}
  +\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{C}_{i+1/2,j},
  \label{eq:semidiscrete-point-value}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
  D_{i+1/2,j}^{\mathrm{up}}
  =\begin{cases}
  \displaystyle
  \frac{2f_{i-1/2,j}-6\avg{f}_{i,j}+4f_{i+1/2,j}}{\Delta x},
  & v_j\ge0,\\[2ex]
  \displaystyle
  \frac{-4f_{i+1/2,j}+6\avg{f}_{i+1,j}-2f_{i+3/2,j}}{\Delta x},
  & v_j<0.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:semidiscrete-upwind-derivative}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:semidiscrete-cell-average}--\eqref{eq:semidiscrete-upwind-derivative} define the same active flux degrees of freedom as the characteristic update, but contain no time-step-dependent coefficient. The two-dimensional counterpart evaluates the normal derivative of the upwind biparabolic reconstruction at every boundary node and retains the conservative edge quadrature in the cell-average component.

For a non-stiff calculation, an $s$-stage explicit Runge--Kutta method can now be applied without changing the spatial operator,
\begin{align}
  \vct{F}^{(k)}
  & =\vct{F}^{n}
  +\Delta t\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}a_{k\ell}
  \mathcal{R}_h\left(\vct{F}^{(\ell)}\right),
  \label{eq:explicit-rk-stage}\\
  \vct{F}^{n+1}
  & =\vct{F}^{n}
  +\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{s}b_k
  \mathcal{R}_h\left(\vct{F}^{(k)}\right).
  \label{eq:explicit-rk-update}
\end{align}
The Julia implementation stores $\vct{F}_h$ as a dense velocity-by-degree-of-freedom array and provides in-place functions with the signature \texttt{rhs!(dF,F,p,t)}. The combined operator is supplied to an \texttt{ODEProblem}; alternatively, collision and transport are supplied separately to a \texttt{SplitODEProblem}. This interface allows, for example, an adaptive explicit Runge--Kutta method for rarefied flow and a stiffly accurate implicit--explicit method for small $\varepsilon$, while enforcing a common molecular transport CFL bound.

\subsection{Dissipation and dispersion of the transport discretization}
\label{sec:transport-spectrum}

The transport part of the discrete-velocity Boltzmann equation is diagonal in molecular velocity. Its spectral properties can therefore be determined from one scalar advection component and then applied independently to every velocity node. Consider a positive velocity $a=v_j>0$ on a uniform periodic mesh, suppress the collision term, and write $q_\ell=f_{\ell+1/2,j}$ for the shared point value. For the Fourier ansatz
\begin{equation}
  \begin{pmatrix}\avg{f}_{\ell,j}\\q_\ell\end{pmatrix}
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}\widehat{f}\\\widehat{q}\end{pmatrix}
  \exp(\mathrm{i}\ell\theta),
  \qquad 0\leq\theta\leq\pi,
  \label{eq:active-flux-fourier-ansatz}
\end{equation}
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:semidiscrete-cell-average} and \eqref{eq:semidiscrete-upwind-derivative} reduce to
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd t}
  \begin{pmatrix}\widehat{f}\\\widehat{q}\end{pmatrix}
  =-\frac{a}{\Delta x}\mathsf{A}(\theta)
  \begin{pmatrix}\widehat{f}\\\widehat{q}\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  \mathsf{A}(\theta)=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0 & 1-e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}\\
    -6 & 4+2e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \label{eq:active-flux-fourier-symbol}
\end{equation}
For $a<0$, upwinding produces the complex-conjugate symbol after reflection of the grid, so the following dissipation and phase-speed conclusions are unchanged. The presence of two active flux degrees of freedom gives two eigenbranches. The eigenvalues $\mu$ of $\mathsf{A}$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \mu^2-\left(4+2e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}\right)\mu
  +6\left(1-e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}\right)=0.
  \label{eq:active-flux-dispersion-relation}
\end{equation}
The root that vanishes at $\theta=0$ is the physical branch. Its long-wave expansion is
\begin{equation}
  \mu_{\mathrm{phy}}
  =\mathrm{i}\theta+\frac{\theta^4}{72}
  +\mathrm{i}\frac{\theta^5}{270}
  -\frac{\theta^6}{648}+O(\theta^7).
  \label{eq:active-flux-physical-expansion}
\end{equation}
Since the corresponding semi-discrete eigenvalue is $\lambda_{\mathrm{phy}}=-a\mu_{\mathrm{phy}}/\Delta x$ and $\theta=k\Delta x$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:active-flux-physical-expansion} gives
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_{\mathrm{phy}}
  =-\mathrm{i}ak
  -\frac{a}{72}k^4\Delta x^3
  -\mathrm{i}\frac{a}{270}k^5\Delta x^4
  +O(\Delta x^5).
  \label{eq:active-flux-modified-wavenumber}
\end{equation}
Thus the leading spatial error is a dissipative fourth-derivative term of order $\Delta x^3$, while the first dispersive correction is of order $\Delta x^4$. This directly recovers third-order transport accuracy without assuming a particular time integrator.

The second root represents an inconsistency between the cell average and interface value rather than an additional physical wave. Its expansion is
\begin{equation}
  \mu_{\mathrm{int}}
  =6-3\mathrm{i}\theta-\theta^2
  +\mathrm{i}\frac{\theta^3}{3}
  +\frac{5\theta^4}{72}+O(\theta^5).
  \label{eq:active-flux-internal-expansion}
\end{equation}
Consequently its decay rate tends to $6a/\Delta x$ in the long-wave limit. A scalar modified-wavenumber curve alone would omit this internal active flux branch, even though that branch can determine the admissible explicit time step.

For a Runge--Kutta method with stability function $R(z)$, the fully discrete amplification matrix and its two modal factors are
\begin{equation}
  \mathsf{G}(\theta,c)
  =R\!\left[-c\mathsf{A}(\theta)\right],
  \qquad
  g_r(\theta,c)=R\!\left[-c\mu_r(\theta)\right],
  \qquad
  c=\frac{|a|\Delta t}{\Delta x},
  \label{eq:active-flux-amplification-matrix}
\end{equation}
where $r\in\{\mathrm{phy},\mathrm{int}\}$. For SSPRK33,
\begin{equation}
  R(z)=1+z+\frac{z^2}{2}+\frac{z^3}{6}.
  \label{eq:ssprk33-stability-function}
\end{equation}
The normalized numerical phase speed and damping rate of the physical branch are defined by
\begin{equation}
  \frac{a_{\mathrm{num}}}{a}
  =-\frac{\arg g_{\mathrm{phy}}}{c\theta},
  \qquad
  d_{\mathrm{num}}
  =-\frac{\log|g_{\mathrm{phy}}|}{c}.
  \label{eq:active-flux-phase-damping}
\end{equation}
Their semi-discrete limits are $\operatorname{Im}(\mu_{\mathrm{phy}})/\theta$ and $\operatorname{Re}(\mu_{\mathrm{phy}})$, respectively.

Figure~\ref{fig:transport-spectrum} shows these quantities for three molecular CFL numbers. Every physical curve approaches unit phase speed and zero damping as $\theta\to0$, consistently with Eq.~\eqref{eq:active-flux-physical-expansion}. The physical branch remains mildly over-dispersive over the resolved spectrum, while damping increases toward the Nyquist mode and suppresses unresolved oscillations. Time integration adds damping to the semi-discrete operator; its influence is small for slow molecular velocities and becomes appreciable as $c$ approaches the stability boundary. The internal mode is damped for all three displayed CFL values. Its attenuation is non-monotone in $c$, however, because the SSPRK33 stability polynomial approaches the negative-real-axis boundary. A numerical maximization of $\rho(\mathsf{G})$ over $0\leq\theta\leq\pi$ gives
\begin{equation}
  c\leq c_{\max}=0.4096
  \label{eq:ssprk33-active-flux-cfl}
\end{equation}
for the unlimited semi-discrete active flux operator. The most restrictive internal mode occurs at $\theta/\pi\simeq0.2766$, so examining only $\theta=0$ would overestimate the allowable step.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{figures/transport-spectrum.pdf}
  \caption{Dissipation and dispersion of the one-dimensional unlimited active flux transport operator for positive molecular velocity. The semi-discrete physical branch is compared with SSPRK33 at molecular CFL numbers $c=0.1$, $0.25$, and $0.4$. Panel (a) shows the phase-speed ratio, panel (b) the normalized physical-mode damping, and panel (c) the amplification magnitude of the internal cell/point mode. Negative velocities give the same curves after grid reflection.}
  \label{fig:transport-spectrum}

In a kinetic calculation the global step is selected from the largest resolved molecular speed, but each velocity node experiences
\begin{equation}
  c_j=\frac{|v_j|}{v_{\max}}\cfl.
  \label{eq:molecular-cfl}
\end{equation}
The fully discrete transport error is therefore velocity dependent even though the same active flux stencil is used for every $j$. Slow particles approach the semi-discrete curves because they are temporally over-resolved, whereas the fastest particles determine stability. The analysis deliberately excludes collision and limiting: Boltzmann or BGK collision contributes physical relaxation and couples velocity components, while a shock limiter makes the transport operator nonlinear. Figure~\ref{fig:transport-spectrum} therefore isolates the numerical dissipation and dispersion supplied by the smooth transport discretization; discontinuous and collision-dominated behavior is assessed by the kinetic test cases in Section~\ref{sec:numerical}.

\subsection{Boltzmann collision evolution and BGK specialization}

The method-of-lines equations advance transport and collision through one additive or partitioned right-hand side. A complementary characteristic implementation composes a transport map $\mathcal{T}$ with a local collision map $\mathcal{C}$. The spatial-convergence programs retain this characteristic path with exact BGK relaxation, while the Sod driver places an OrdinaryDiffEq Runge--Kutta transport solve between two successive-penalty full-Boltzmann collision half steps. In this composed path, a second-order Strang sequence has the form \cite{strang1968construction}
\begin{equation}
  f^{n+1}
  = \mathcal{C}_{\Delta t/2}
    \mathcal{T}_{\Delta t}
    \mathcal{C}_{\Delta t/2}f^n.
  \label{eq:strang}
\end{equation}
For the full Boltzmann equation, which remains the target model, $\mathcal{C}_{\delta t}$ advances the spatially homogeneous system
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial f_{\vct{j}}}{\partial t}
  =\mathcal{Q}_{h,\vct{j}}(f,f),
  \label{eq:homogeneous-boltzmann}
\end{equation}
independently at every physical-space evaluation point. The target implementation evaluates $\mathcal{Q}_h$ with a fast spectral method, which reduces the cost of the velocity-space convolution while retaining spectral resolution of a general collision kernel \cite{mouhot2006fast,xiao2021flux}. An explicit collision integrator is sufficient in rarefied regimes; an ESDIRK or penalized implicit--explicit treatment is required when the collision term becomes stiff. This collision module is independent of whether the physical mesh is one- or two-dimensional.

For the BGK specialization, the conservative moments, Maxwellian, and collision time remain constant during a collision-only substep. The local solution is therefore available exactly,
\begin{equation}
  f(t+\delta t)
  = \mathcal{M}
  + \exp(-\delta t/\tau)\left[f(t)-\mathcal{M}\right].
  \label{eq:exact-relaxation}
\end{equation}

For either collision model, applying the collision map directly to $\avg{f}_i$ would not approximate the cell average of the nonlinear collision term to the design order. The reconstructed distribution is instead evaluated at physical-space Gauss--Legendre points, the collision problem is advanced independently at every point, and the evolved distributions are integrated back to the cell average. Four Gauss points are used in each one-dimensional verification cell. The two-dimensional formulation uses the corresponding tensor-product quadrature of Eq.~\eqref{eq:biparabolic-reconstruction}. Boundary point distributions are collided pointwise after all cell reconstructions have been processed. This ordering ensures that every cell uses a common old-time boundary state.

\subsection{Discrete collision conservation}
\label{sec:collision-conservation}

Velocity truncation and spectral evaluation can leave a small compatibility defect in the raw Boltzmann collision vector $\mathcal{Q}_{h,\vct{j}}^*$. Define
\begin{equation}
  \vct{d}_{\mathcal{Q}}
  =\sum_{\vct{j}}\omega_{\vct{j}}
  \mathcal{Q}_{h,\vct{j}}^*\vct{\psi}_{\vct{j}}.
  \label{eq:collision-defect}
\end{equation}
A minimum-dimensional moment projection is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{Q}_{h,\vct{j}}^c
  =\mathcal{Q}_{h,\vct{j}}^*
  -\vct{\alpha}_{\mathcal{Q}}^T\vct{\psi}_{\vct{j}},
  \qquad
  \vct{G}_{\mathcal{Q}}\vct{\alpha}_{\mathcal{Q}}
  =\vct{d}_{\mathcal{Q}},
  \qquad
  \vct{G}_{\mathcal{Q}}
  =\sum_{\vct{j}}\omega_{\vct{j}}
  \vct{\psi}_{\vct{j}}\vct{\psi}_{\vct{j}}^T.
  \label{eq:collision-projection}
\end{equation}
It follows directly that $\sum_{\vct{j}}\omega_{\vct{j}}\mathcal{Q}_{h,\vct{j}}^c\vct{\psi}_{\vct{j}}=\vct{0}$. The correction acts only in velocity space and can therefore be applied identically at all one- and two-dimensional physical-space quadrature points.

For the BGK model, conservation is imposed by correcting the discrete Maxwellian rather than the collision vector. On a finite velocity interval, quadrature of the analytic Maxwellian in Eq.~\eqref{eq:maxwellian} introduces a small moment defect. Repeated relaxation toward this function would cause the discrete collision invariants to drift. Let
\begin{equation}
  \vct{d}
  = \vct{W}_h
  -\sum_{\vct{j}}\omega_{\vct{j}}
  \mathcal{M}_{\vct{j}}\vct{\psi}_{\vct{j}}.
  \label{eq:moment-defect}
\end{equation}
A corrected equilibrium is defined by
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{M}_{d,\vct{j}}
  = \mathcal{M}_{\vct{j}}
  \left(1+\vct{\alpha}^{T}\vct{\psi}_{\vct{j}}\right).
  \label{eq:discrete-maxwellian}
\end{equation}
The correction coefficients follow from a $(d_v+2)\times(d_v+2)$ system, which reduces to $3\times3$ in the present \texttt{1d1f1v} tests,
\begin{equation}
  \vct{G}\vct{\alpha}=\vct{d},
  \qquad
  \vct{G}=\sum_{\vct{j}}\omega_{\vct{j}}\mathcal{M}_{\vct{j}}
  \vct{\psi}_{\vct{j}}\vct{\psi}_{\vct{j}}^{T}.
  \label{eq:moment-correction}
\end{equation}
Substitution into Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete-moments} shows that $\mathcal{M}_d$ reproduces $\vct{W}_h$ exactly up to the linear-solver tolerance. The correction is small when the velocity interval resolves the tails of the distribution. In the numerical experiments it reduces the global collision-invariant drift from approximately $10^{-11}$ to $10^{-13}$ or below.

\subsection{Hydrodynamic limit and asymptotic-preserving design}
\label{sec:ap-analysis}

An asymptotic-preserving (AP) discretization must remain stable when $\varepsilon\ll1$ without resolving the collision time and, at fixed $\Delta x$ and $\Delta t$, must reduce to a consistent discretization of the limiting macroscopic equations as $\varepsilon\to0$ \cite{filbet2010ap,dimarco2013imex}. These two requirements distinguish AP behavior from collision stability alone. Applying an explicit Runge--Kutta method to Eq.~\eqref{eq:semidiscrete-system} generally imposes $\Delta t=O(\varepsilon)$. Exact BGK relaxation or penalized Boltzmann collision can remove that stability restriction, but a single Filbet--Jin penalty step has only a well-prepared, or weak, AP property. The successive-penalty map used below restores a strong homogeneous relaxation limit for non-equilibrium input \cite{yan2013successive}. Its composition with shock-limited transport still does not by itself establish uniform accuracy of the complete spatial--temporal scheme. The equilibrium-null-space argument is collision-model independent: both the Boltzmann and BGK operators share the Maxwellian equilibrium manifold. We first state the algebraic conditions for a general $\mathcal{C}_h$, use BGK to exhibit them transparently, and then return to the penalized full Boltzmann construction.

For the method-of-lines system, consider an implicit--explicit Runge--Kutta discretization in which transport is explicit and collision is implicit,
\begin{equation}
  \vct{F}^{(k)}
  =\vct{F}^{n}
  +\Delta t\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}a^{E}_{k\ell}
     \mathcal{T}_h\left(\vct{F}^{(\ell)}\right)
  +\frac{\Delta t}{\varepsilon}
   \sum_{\ell=1}^{k}a^{I}_{k\ell}
     \mathcal{C}_h\left(\vct{F}^{(\ell)}\right).
  \label{eq:imex-stage}
\end{equation}
Let $\mathcal{P}_h$ denote the discrete velocity-moment map in Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete-moments}. The collision construction must satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{P}_h\mathcal{C}_h(\vct{F})=\vct{0},
  \qquad
  \mathcal{C}_h(\vct{F})=\vct{0}
  \Longleftrightarrow
  \vct{F}=\mathcal{E}_h(\vct{W}),
  \quad \vct{W}=\mathcal{P}_h\vct{F},
  \label{eq:discrete-equilibrium-nullspace}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{E}_h$ is a discrete equilibrium map. For the BGK model, the AP-oriented implementation uses the degree-of-freedom projection
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{C}_{h,a}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\vct{F})
  =\frac{
  \mathcal{M}_d[\mathcal{P}_h\vct{F}_a]-\vct{F}_a}
  {\widehat{\tau}_a},
  \label{eq:equilibrium-projection-collision}
\end{equation}
for every cell-average or boundary-point degree of freedom $a$, with $\tau_a=\varepsilon\widehat{\tau}_a$. The corrected Maxwellian in Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete-maxwellian} makes both identities in Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete-equilibrium-nullspace} hold under the numerical velocity quadrature.

Assume that the implicit tableau has nonzero diagonal entries and that the additive method is globally stiffly accurate. For well-prepared data, taking $\varepsilon\to0$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:imex-stage} at fixed discretization forces
\begin{equation}
  \vct{F}^{(k)}
  =\mathcal{E}_h\left(\vct{W}^{(k)}\right).
  \label{eq:imex-equilibrium-stage}
\end{equation}
Application of $\mathcal{P}_h$ eliminates every implicit collision term and gives
\begin{equation}
  \vct{W}^{(k)}
  =\vct{W}^{n}
  +\Delta t\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}a^{E}_{k\ell}
  \mathcal{P}_h\mathcal{T}_h
  \left(\mathcal{E}_h(\vct{W}^{(\ell)})\right).
  \label{eq:imex-euler-limit}
\end{equation}
This is an explicit Runge--Kutta discretization of the equilibrium kinetic flux, and hence a consistent kinetic flux-vector-splitting approximation of the compressible Euler limit. Its stability step is controlled by molecular transport rather than by $\varepsilon$. Equations~\eqref{eq:imex-equilibrium-stage} and \eqref{eq:imex-euler-limit} establish the formal Euler-limit AP property of the equilibrium-projection option when it is paired with a suitable stiffly accurate implicit--explicit method.

There remains an important accuracy distinction. The physical-space quadrature collision in the legacy solver approximates the cell average of the nonlinear source to high order at finite Knudsen number, but reconstructing a distribution and applying a nonlinear equilibrium map do not commute. Its discrete null space is therefore not exactly Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete-equilibrium-nullspace} at fixed $\Delta x$. Conversely, the degree-of-freedom projection in Eq.~\eqref{eq:equilibrium-projection-collision} supplies the exact equilibrium manifold needed for the AP limit, but is not yet a uniformly high-order approximation of the finite-$\varepsilon$ cell-averaged source. A macro--micro reconstruction of the form
\begin{equation}
  f_h=\mathcal{M}_d[\vct{W}_h]+\varepsilon g_h,
  \qquad \mathcal{P}_h g_h=\vct{0},
  \label{eq:macro-micro-reconstruction}
\end{equation}
is the planned route to combine design-order kinetic accuracy, an exact discrete equilibrium manifold, and uniform accuracy in $\varepsilon$.

For the full Boltzmann operator, a direct nonlinear implicit solve at every physical quadrature point is prohibitively expensive. A standard AP construction introduces a BGK penalty \cite{filbet2010ap,dimarco2013imex},
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{Q}_h(f,f)
  =\left[\mathcal{Q}_h(f,f)
    -\mu\left(\mathcal{M}_d[f]-f\right)\right]
   +\mu\left(\mathcal{M}_d[f]-f\right),
  \label{eq:boltzmann-penalization}
\end{equation}
where the bracketed residual is explicit and the BGK penalty is implicit. The conservative projection in Eq.~\eqref{eq:collision-projection} must be applied before the split so that both the residual and penalty have compatible moments. A single implicit penalty denominator remains bounded but does not strongly project general non-equilibrium input onto the Maxwellian as $\varepsilon\to0$. The Sod implementation therefore uses two successive implicit BGK penalty stages \cite{yan2013successive}. With $\lambda^n=\delta t/\tau^n$, a fixed $0<\alpha<1$, and $\mathcal{M}_d^n$ determined by the collision invariants, eliminating those two stages gives
\begin{equation}
  f^{n+1}-\mathcal{M}_d^n
  =\frac{(1+\lambda^n)(f^n-\mathcal{M}_d^n)
  +\delta t\,\mathcal{Q}_h(f^n,f^n)}
  {(1+\alpha\lambda^n)
   [1+(1-\alpha)\lambda^n]},
  \label{eq:sod-penalized-collision}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha=1/2$ is used below. For $\lambda^n\ll1$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:sod-penalized-collision} reduces to $f^{n+1}=f^n+\delta t\,\mathcal{Q}_h(f^n,f^n)+O(\delta t^2)$. For $\lambda^n\to\infty$, its numerator is linear and its denominator quadratic in $\lambda^n$, so $f^{n+1}-\mathcal{M}_d^n=O(1/\lambda^n)$ even for non-well-prepared input. The complete conservative FSM value is evaluated once at every collision call, and a subsequent positive exponential projection preserves the same five discrete moments. Uniform accuracy of the Strang-composed active flux method remains to be verified separately.

\subsection{Discontinuities, positivity, and boundaries}

The unlimited quadratic reconstruction is not suitable near an under-resolved shock. In one dimension, for each velocity component, a normalized kinetic jump is evaluated at an interface,
\begin{equation}
  s_{i+1/2,j}
  = \frac{|\avg{f}_{i+1,j}-\avg{f}_{i,j}|}
  {|\avg{f}_{i+1,j}|+|\avg{f}_{i,j}|+\epsilon}.
  \label{eq:jump-sensor}
\end{equation}
The high-order active flux point value and time average are blended with their first-order upwind counterparts through
\begin{equation}
  \theta_{i+1/2,j}
  = \operatorname{clamp}\left(
  \frac{s_{\max}-s_{i+1/2,j}}{s_{\max}-s_{\min}},0,1\right),
  \quad s_{\min}=0.02,
  \quad s_{\max}=0.20.
  \label{eq:shock-blend}
\end{equation}
Thus, the active flux update is retained in smooth regions and transitions continuously to kinetic upwinding at a strong jump. The first-order cell-average candidate is positive under Eq.~\eqref{eq:cfl}. If an antidiffusive correction would produce a negative cell average, the correction is scaled conservatively toward that positive candidate. Negative interface point values are projected to zero; this operation does not modify the finite-volume balance because point values are additional, nonconservative degrees of freedom. The collision reconstruction is contracted toward its positive cell average whenever a Gauss-point value is negative, following the same convex-limiting principle used in positivity-preserving high-order methods \cite{hu2013positivity,kiechle2025positivity}.

On a two-dimensional face, the same sensor is formed from the two adjacent cell averages and the blending is applied to all three face point values. The first-order reference state is selected by the sign of $\vct{v}_{x,\vct{j}}\cdot\vct{n}_e$. This face-normal construction reduces exactly to Eqs.~\eqref{eq:jump-sensor}--\eqref{eq:shock-blend} in one dimension; a multidimensional bound-preserving proof and numerical assessment remain necessary for the complete two-dimensional implementation.

Periodic boundaries are imposed by copying cell and boundary-point ghosts. At an open boundary with outward normal $\vct{n}$, a prescribed reservoir is used only for incoming characteristics satisfying $\vct{v}_x\cdot\vct{n}<0$; outgoing values are evolved from the interior. In the one-dimensional shock tube, this condition gives $v_j>0$ at the left boundary and $v_j<0$ at the right boundary.

The implementation exposes two time-integration paths. The characteristic--Strang path retained by the spatial-convergence programs is summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item reconstruct the old-time distribution at one-dimensional or tensor-product physical-space quadrature points;
  \item apply a half collision step using either the conservative Boltzmann operator or the exact BGK relaxation;
  \item evolve all boundary point values along molecular characteristics and compute the one-dimensional exact time average or two-dimensional space--time Simpson average;
  \item update cell averages with the conservative kinetic flux balance;
  \item repeat the reconstructed collision evolution over a half step;
  \item refresh discrete velocity moments and boundary ghosts.
\end{enumerate}
For the method-of-lines path, the state is first packed into cell-average and shared-point blocks. Each call to the right-hand side then evaluates Eqs.~\eqref{eq:semidiscrete-cell-average}--\eqref{eq:semidiscrete-upwind-derivative}, adds either the quadrature collision or equilibrium-projection collision, and applies the boundary closure. An ordinary differential equation solver controls the Runge--Kutta stages and time-step acceptance. In the split problem, the stiff collision function is listed first and transport second, following the implicit--explicit convention of \texttt{OrdinaryDiffEq.jl}. The smooth periodic case implements this complete interface in \texttt{advection.jl}. The shock tube implements its case-specific limited transport right-hand side in \texttt{sod.jl}; conservative two-stage successive-penalty FSM half steps surround an \texttt{SSPRK33} solve of that right-hand side. The three production jobs are exposed separately by \texttt{sod\_kn1e-4.jl}, \texttt{sod\_kn1e-2.jl}, and \texttt{sod\_kn1.jl}; each writes one portable JLD2 state, while \texttt{sod\_plot.jl} reads those states without loading the solver. The shared normal-shock solver \texttt{shock.jl} packs the \texttt{1d1f3v} distribution in the same way and reuses one explicit-Euler \texttt{ODEProblem} transport step for both collision models. Reconstructed collision half steps dispatch either to the full FSM operator or to exact BGK relaxation, so changing the collision model does not alter the spatial transport path. Euler integration is selected for this preliminary, collision-dominated comparison to minimize transport-stage cost rather than to pursue high temporal accuracy. Because FSM evaluation dominates the wall time, the executable workflow is divided into \texttt{shock\_boltzmann.jl}, \texttt{shock\_bgk.jl}, and \texttt{shock\_plot.jl}: the first two independently save portable JLD2 data, while the third reads those files and produces the comparison without initializing either solver.

\section{Numerical experiments}
\label{sec:numerical}

The validation is arranged as four cases of increasing physical and numerical complexity. The accuracy case uses the \texttt{1d1f1v} BGK specialization. Its two convergence programs deliberately retain characteristic active-flux transport and temporal over-resolution so that their tables continue to measure spatial accuracy. The standalone periodic driver uses the complete \texttt{OrdinaryDiffEq.jl} interface. The Sod case changes to a \texttt{1d1f3v} full-Boltzmann model and compares three reference Knudsen numbers with the same transport discretization. The third case also uses \texttt{1d1f3v} and directly compares the full fast-spectral Boltzmann collision integral with BGK. Its present calculation is deliberately short and coarse, so it verifies the implementation and reveals initial model sensitivity but is not presented as a converged steady shock. The lid-driven cavity remains a fully specified two-dimensional target. A strong-AP homogeneous collision map is exercised down to $\mathrm{Kn}=10^{-4}$, but uniform-in-Knudsen accuracy evidence for the complete scheme is not yet reported as a completed result.

\subsubsection{Temporally over-resolved BGK test}

The implemented BGK method combines third-order active flux transport with second-order Strang splitting. With a fixed molecular $\cfl$, $\Delta t=O(\Delta x)$ and the $O(\Delta t^2)$ splitting error masks third-order spatial convergence. To expose the spatial behavior while retaining Strang splitting, the time step is refined as
\begin{equation}
  \Delta t = C\frac{\Delta x^{3/2}}{v_{\max}},
  \label{eq:overresolved-step}
\end{equation}
so the global splitting error scales as $O(\Delta x^3)$. A uniform step is chosen to divide the final time exactly, avoiding a mesh-dependent shortened last step.

The periodic wave in Eq.~\eqref{eq:wave-initial} is evolved with $\mathrm{Kn}=10^{-2}$, $N_v=24$, and $t=0.005$. Since no closed-form BGK solution is available, an $N_x=640$ solution is restricted conservatively onto every comparison grid. On the finest refinement, the distribution and density orders are $3.076$ and $3.002$, showing compatible third-order behavior. The slight value above three for the distribution is attributable to the finite-grid reference and pre-asymptotic error cancellation.

\subsection{Case 3: Normal shock structure}
\label{sec:normal-shock}

The normal shock provides a direct full-Boltzmann/BGK collision-model comparison. It is stationary in the shock-attached frame and resolves a non-equilibrium layer whose thickness is comparable to the molecular mean free path. In the present normalization the upstream primitive state is $\rho_1=1$, $\lambda_1=1$, $p_1=T_1=1/2$, and $U_1=\mathrm{Ma}_1\sqrt{\gamma/2}$. For a monatomic gas with a three-dimensional molecular velocity, $K=0$ and $\gamma=5/3$. The downstream equilibrium state follows from the Rankine--Hugoniot relations,
\begin{align}
  \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}
  & =\frac{(\gamma+1)\mathrm{Ma}_1^2}
  {(\gamma-1)\mathrm{Ma}_1^2+2},
  &
  \frac{U_2}{U_1}
  & =\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2},
  \label{eq:normal-shock-density}\\
  \frac{p_2}{p_1}
  & =1+\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}
  \left(\mathrm{Ma}_1^2-1\right),
  &
  T_2
  & =\frac{p_2\rho_1}{p_1\rho_2}T_1.
  \label{eq:normal-shock-pressure}
\end{align}
Incoming particles at the left and right boundaries are assigned the corresponding upstream and downstream discrete Maxwellians. The full collision integral uses the variable-hard-sphere fast spectral method \cite{mouhot2006fast,xiao2021flux} and the conservative projection in Eq.~\eqref{eq:collision-projection}. The same active flux transport right-hand side is integrated by explicit Euler through \texttt{ODEProblem} for both the Boltzmann and BGK calculations; only the local collision routine changes. Mach numbers two and three remain the target steady tests and allow direct comparison with the earlier flux reconstruction Boltzmann study \cite{xiao2021flux}.

The two collision models are deliberately run as separate jobs. The full-Boltzmann driver writes \texttt{data/shock\_boltzmann.jld2}, and the BGK driver writes \texttt{data/shock\_bgk.jld2}. Each file contains the physical and velocity grids, cell and interface distributions, conservative and primitive variables, reservoir states, scalar diagnostics, and the derived one-dimensional profiles. Session-specific solver objects and the large precomputed FSM kernel are omitted. A third program validates and compares these files to generate Fig.~\ref{fig:normal-shock-preliminary}; changing plot styles or diagnostics therefore does not repeat the costly collision calculation.

The finite Fourier grid gives a small nonzero raw value of $\mathcal{Q}_h(\mathcal{M}_d,\mathcal{M}_d)$. The implementation therefore evaluates the well-balanced difference $\mathcal{Q}_h(f,f)-\mathcal{Q}_h(\mathcal{M}_d,\mathcal{M}_d)$ before applying the five-invariant projection. An explicit midpoint collision update is sufficient at $\mathrm{Kn}=1$. Small negative Fourier undershoots are removed by an exponential moment projection: after clipping, $f$ is multiplied by $\exp(\vct{\alpha}\cdot\vct{\psi})$, where the five coefficients are obtained by Newton iteration so that mass, three momentum components, and energy return to their pre-collision values. \section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Kinetic equations provide a direct connection between particle transport and macroscopic flow evolution, but their phase-space dimensionality places strong demands on numerical resolution. In this paper, an active flux kinetic formulation has been developed from the discrete-velocity Boltzmann equation on one- and two-dimensional Cartesian physical domains. Cell averages and shared boundary point values define compact continuous reconstructions, while molecular characteristics evolve the point values and provide conservative time-averaged fluxes. In two dimensions, a biparabolic reconstruction and space--time Simpson quadrature extend the construction without dimensional splitting. The collision interface admits a conservative fast spectral Boltzmann operator and the BGK model as a simplified specialization. Thus, the use of BGK in the accuracy verification is a validation choice rather than a restriction of the physical-space method. A semi-discrete form separates the active flux spatial right-hand side from temporal integration and supports both ordinary and split differential-equation problems. Its two-branch Fourier spectrum identifies a third-order physical transport mode and a rapidly damped internal cell/point mode; after Runge--Kutta integration, the latter sets a stricter CFL limit than a physical-mode analysis alone.

The numerical programme is organized into four cases. The completed one-dimensional accuracy experiment identifies third-order convergence of both the distribution and density. With temporal over-resolution, the retained characteristic--Strang benchmark reaches the same spatial rate despite second-order splitting. The standalone periodic BGK case verifies the complete \texttt{ODEProblem} interface. The fine-grid Sod calculation then exercises the full FSM collision integral in \texttt{1d1f3v} at three Knudsen numbers; its shock-limited \texttt{SSPRK33} transport captures the principal wave structures, and the successive collision penalty produces clear macroscopic and heat-flux separation between the near-equilibrium $\mathrm{Kn}=10^{-4}$ result and the kinetic results. The preliminary normal shock uses the same projected FSM machinery in a direct comparison with BGK and identifies heat flux as the earlier discriminator between collision models. A steady refined normal shock, systematic discontinuous-regime mesh sensitivity, and the genuinely two-dimensional lid-driven cavity remain necessary before quantitative physical validation is claimed.

The asymptotic analysis also clarifies the status of the time discretization. An explicit Runge--Kutta method applied to the complete kinetic right-hand side is not AP because it resolves the collision time. In contrast to the former single-denominator update, the two-stage penalized full-Boltzmann collision map used by the Sod driver has a strong homogeneous AP limit: for arbitrary non-equilibrium input, its microscopic component vanishes as the collision time tends to zero. Its Strang composition with the shock-limited transport solver does not alone establish uniform accuracy or convergence to the discrete Euler solution, however. The equilibrium-projection BGK source paired with a stiffly accurate implicit--explicit method is formally AP at the Euler level; a macro--micro reconstruction is still needed to combine this property with uniform high-order finite-Knudsen accuracy. For the full Boltzmann equation, the corresponding long-term target is a stage-coupled conservative BGK penalization rather than a direct nonlinear implicit collision solve.

The immediate research tasks are therefore part of the central scope rather than optional extensions: use a fixed physical and temporal grid to quantify the complete scheme uniformly in Knudsen number, extend the collision-level successive penalty to a globally stiffly accurate full-Boltzmann implicit--explicit method, quantify mesh sensitivity for discontinuous kinetic flows, extend the preliminary normal shock to steady Mach-two and Mach-three profiles, and complete the lid-driven cavity results in Section~\ref{sec:lid-driven-cavity}. Less dissipative bound-preserving limiting and gas-surface interaction can then be assessed within the resulting one- and two-dimensional multiscale framework.

\section*{Data and code availability}

The Julia programs in the \texttt{src/} directory retain targeted, case-specific entry points. The file \texttt{advection.jl} contains the periodic BGK data, semi-discrete transport and collision right-hand sides, and the corresponding \texttt{ODEProblem} and optional \texttt{SplitODEProblem}. The standalone \texttt{dispersion.jl} program evaluates both eigenbranches of the same semi-discrete transport symbol, determines the SSPRK33 stability limit, and regenerates Fig.~\ref{fig:transport-spectrum}. For the shock tube, \texttt{sod.jl} supplies the self-contained \texttt{1d1f3v} data, FSM collision workspace, limiter, boundary treatment, and case-specific transport \texttt{ODEProblem}. Three \texttt{sod\_kn*.jl} launchers independently write compressed JLD2 results, while \texttt{sod\_plot.jl} compares only their saved arrays. For the computationally heavier third case, \texttt{shock.jl} supplies the shared \texttt{1d1f3v} solver, \texttt{shock\_boltzmann.jl} and \texttt{shock\_bgk.jl} independently write compressed JLD2 results, and \texttt{shock\_plot.jl} compares only the saved arrays. The programs \texttt{convergence1-transport.jl} and \texttt{convergence2-overresolve.jl} retain their characteristic algorithms solely for the spatial-accuracy measurements. A conservative two-stage successive-penalty full-Boltzmann collision map is implemented for Sod; a uniformly accurate AP integrator and the two-dimensional solver described by the general formulation have not yet been added. The LaTeX manuscript, generated figures, and portable numerical data are provided with the project. No external experimental data are required.
